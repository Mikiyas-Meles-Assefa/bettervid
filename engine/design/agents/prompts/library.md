## Library: what research and craft know (consult it; it is knowledge, not rules)

Attention
- A thing that starts to move, appears or changes pulls the eye for about a second; ongoing motion pulls far less
  (Abrams and Christ 2003). Right after a cut, motion leads where eyes go (Carmi and Itti 2006; Smith and Mital 2013).
- Size, contrast and nearness to the center also pull. Words and pictures draw more looks than plain shapes.
- A cue that points at what is needed next (a highlight, a pointer, a dim on the rest) helps learning, about d = 0.4
  to 0.5 (Schneider et al. 2018). A cue on the wrong thing costs.

Load
- People hold about 3 to 4 objects in visual working memory (Luck and Vogel 1997).
- Changes during a cut, a camera move or another strong change are often missed (Rensink et al. 1997).
- Decoration that does not carry the idea costs learning (Rey 2012).
- Things that move together read as one (common fate); a stream of look-alike marks reads as one stream.

Timing to the voice
- The eye reaches a named thing about 200 to 400 ms after its word starts (Allopenna et al. 1998).
- A picture may lead its words a little; it should not trail them (contiguity, about d = 0.85, Ginns 2006).
- On-screen text that repeats the narration word for word hurts learning; short labels help (Adesope and Nesbit 2012).
- Timed text is read at about 12 to 17 characters a second; slower for children and second-language viewers
  (Szarkowska and Gerber-Moron 2018; the Netflix timed text guide).

Animation and pacing
- Animation helps when the change shown is the change in the idea, and slow enough to see (Tversky et al. 2002;
  Hoffler and Leutner 2007). Segmenting into pieces with pauses helps (Rey et al. 2019).
- An event boundary (a cut, a new scene) clears working memory (Zacks et al. 2010); keep what must be compared on one
  side of it.
- A question asked before the answer helps people remember that answer (St. Hilaire et al. 2023). Hiding something the
  viewer is waiting for builds suspense; showing the audience more than the character knows builds it too (Hitchcock's
  bomb under the table).
- Guidance that helps newcomers bores or slows experts (Kalyuga et al. 2003).

Motion craft
- Stage changes: exits, then moves, then entrances (Heer and Robertson 2007). Keep identity: a thing that changes
  should morph, not vanish and reappear (Manim Transform, Keynote Magic Move).
- Interface motion usually takes 0.2 to 0.5 s, longer for longer distances and larger things; under 0.1 s reads as
  a jump, over 1 s as slow (NN/g; Material 3 motion tokens). Ease out on arrival, ease in on exit.

Physics and perception for demonstrations
- Free motion under gravity over a height h takes sqrt(2h / 9.8) seconds: 0.25 s over 30 cm, 0.45 s over 1 m, 1.4 s
  over 10 m. A door swinging shut, a snap or a splash is over in a fraction of a second.
- Events under about half a second are seen but cannot be inspected. Real speed keeps them believable; a replay at
  about a quarter to a third of real speed lets the eye compare. Slow motion first can make an event look fake.
- A process that takes minutes or days is shown sped up; say so on screen or in the narration.

Reading and legibility
- Size is what reaches the eye: visual angle from the frame's physical width and the viewing distance. Typical
  views: a phone frame about 65 mm wide at 350 to 400 mm; a laptop about 300 mm at 600 mm; a TV about 1200 mm at
  3000 mm; a classroom projection about 2000 mm seen from 8000 mm at the back row.
- Text read on purpose, by a viewer who stops to read, holds its speed above about 18 arcmin of cap height (Legge
  2007). Text read at a glance or in passing (a feed, captions while watching the picture) needs about 27 arcmin. Contrast at least 3:1 for large text, 4.5:1 for body
  text (WCAG 2).
- Fewer type sizes per frame read calmer; one size step between subject and support is often enough.

Platforms and captions
- Feed apps draw their own name, caption, buttons and progress over the frame, usually a band along the bottom and a
  strip down the right in a tall frame (each platform publishes its safe zones). A TV, a lesson played full screen or
  a slide in a meeting has none, but a meeting app may overlay names and controls.
- Many feed viewers start with the sound off, so burned-in captions carry the words for them; with sound on, captions
  repeat the narration (see the redundancy finding) and compete with the frame. Deaf and hard of hearing viewers and
  second-language viewers need captions available. Captions belong where nothing important is drawn.
