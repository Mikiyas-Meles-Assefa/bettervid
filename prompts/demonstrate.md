The viewer hears and sees everything once. A process described in words is heard and forgotten; the same process watched on a concrete instance, one step at a time, is understood. The picture is drawn by an engine that can compute some processes for real and draw anything else (below), and it will show the narration's example with its very values, step by step. So the example the narration chooses is what the viewer will see.

Read the narration as the person who asked, hearing it once, and check:

1. Every process, mechanism or method this viewer needs to understand or do: does the narration work through a concrete instance of it, or only describe it? Name each one that is only described, and where.
2. For each example: does every step the narration explains actually happen in it, visibly, doing real work? An example where a step has nothing to do, a branch is never taken, or the case that makes the method necessary never comes up shows the viewer the wrong thing. An instance where most steps are degenerate (one thing against one thing, nothing to carry, nothing left over, a single pass that already finishes) is technically correct and still does not show the mechanism.
3. Its size. Most processes have a unit that repeats: a step per item, a round, a pass, a pass of a loop, a combining step, a generation. Count how many times that unit happens in full in the example, with every one of its steps doing real work. An instance at its smallest possible case (one thing against one, an inner loop that runs once, a single item where the unit is about many) does not count when the unit's own repetition is what the viewer must learn: it hides that repetition. The video will play the first full instance slowly and fade to faster ones, so plan that fade on the example: which full instance plays slowly, which ones follow faster? The slow one must be a full instance, and at least one more full instance must come after it to play faster. If the only full instance is the last one, the viewer watches the mechanism once, at the end, and never sees it repeat: the example is too small, however correct and tidy it is. At the same time, few enough to follow on a phone screen without losing track.
4. Its values: easy arithmetic, values easy to tell apart, nothing the viewer must strain to compute while the next step arrives, unless the difficulty is the point.
5. Whether the picture can show it truthfully with what the engine can do, and, where a kit computes the process, whether the example fits what that kit takes.
6. For a story or an argument, the concrete instance is the evidence itself: the measurement, the document, the moment. Is the claim that carries the video shown with its evidence, or only asserted?

Judge for this viewer and their reason for watching: what they need to see, not every example one could add. The narration's scope is settled: a detail it mentions in passing does not need its own demonstration, and a note never adds a case, topic or subtlety the viewer did not come for. If the narration already shows its ideas working with good examples, say so. Never ask for a new fact about the world; an example built to show how something works is fine when every step is correct.

Each note must be concrete enough to act on: what to change, where, and to what, with the exact example you would use (its values) and why it shows the idea better.
