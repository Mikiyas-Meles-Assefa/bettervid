## Wave 3: the teaching bench
Judges teaching in time, not stills: 20 to 30 clips of 15 to 40 s across very different uses, each with narration and timing, scenes written by Haiku agents. Measured from scene data and audio: is the eye on the right thing, does the picture keep up with the words, slow first then faster, how much arrives at once, does each pause do its job. Measured by a model: a first-time learner watches the clip as timed frames with the words and is quizzed; a follow test asks what the viewer looks at and waits for at each moment. The user watches a few clips at each milestone.

## Wave 4: the design brain
Its own wave with a lead agent that runs its own sub-agents (composition and layout, type, motion and transitions, camera, direction, Haiku authors). The author says what matters at each moment; the engine composes the frame and its motion like a top motion designer, so a weak author still gets a great frame. Direction (attention, pacing, reveals, pauses) is an ability the engine reasons with, never a recipe per video type, because nobody knows what people will use it for. Every change is scored on the bench.

## Wave 5: what can be shown
The user's own material comes first: most people will bring project files, docs, notes, slides, images and data. Reading that material (planning and intent from it, faithful to it) and composing with their images and content as first-class pieces belong here, not only in the platform wave. Agentic parsing: an agent reads the files with tools (PDF, slides, docs, spreadsheets, code, images), finds structure, claims, steps, figures, tables, equations and data, reads figures with vision, and checks its reading against the source. Bring static material to life, faithfully: a chart image becomes its data and a live chart that draws, highlights and morphs; a diagram splits into parts and arrows animated one link at a time; a photo splits into objects with SAM (depth, parallax, parts that move or get marked); a screenshot gets zoomed regions, highlights and stepped flows; a table fills, sorts and compares; an equation image becomes typeset math that transforms; code runs with real values. The material sets requirements, not only supplies pictures: one part of a project can say what matters in another (a syllabus or rubric marks a chart in the lecture slides as essential, a brand guide fixes colors and logo, a brief names the must-say claims). The planner links documents to each other and turns that into requirements of the video (cover this, show this exact figure, never say that), and the checks verify each one is met. A shot does not just ask for "an image here": the plan knows which of the user's figures must appear and why. Modular so it works for 1 file or 1,000 (NotebookLM quality drops well before its 300-source limit): (1) ingest, one reader per file type, every piece keeps its location; (2) a cached source card per source written by a small agent in parallel (claims with locations, figures and what they show, tables, data, terms), so no model reads everything at once; (3) index over cards, passages and figures; (3b) the project graph, a linker rather than one merged input: nodes are sources and their parts (sections, slides, figures, tables, claims, terms, images, code), edges are relations an agent finds and checks (a syllabus topic covered by a slide, a rubric weight, a brand guide applying to every frame, a newer version replacing an older one, a chart showing a data file, two sources disagreeing), and each source gets a role from what it is and how it is used (content, requirement, constraint, evidence, asset), never from its file name; the planner asks the graph questions, conflicts are flagged, and the graph stays with the project so later videos (episodes, cuts, other audiences) reuse it; (4) agentic planning from the cards, pulling exact passages and figures as needed, with layered summaries for very large sets; (5) grounding, every claim and visual linked to its source location and checked; (6) bring-to-life converters, one per kind, each with a faithfulness check. Each module is its own cached stage with fixed interfaces. Picture finding becomes an agent that hunts like a skilled researcher: searches broadly (the user's files first, web image search, archives and museums such as Wikimedia, Smithsonian, Library of Congress, NASA, Internet Archive, Europeana, free stock such as Openverse, Unsplash, Pexels), looks at results with vision, says what is wrong and rewrites its queries (other names, languages, dates, collections), opens source pages for caption, date, maker and license, prefers open licenses and records credit, treats unclear rights as reference only, and draws or generates in style when nothing honest exists, never passing generated as real. Ranking within that hunt uses meaning: a local image-embedding model (CLIP or SigLIP style, no per-call cost) embeds every candidate once (the user's uploads, figures pulled from their files, archive results); each shot's need is matched against what images show, the user's own material first; a vision model confirms the shortlist; embeddings never decide alone. Truth engines (physics and simulations, charts that morph, maps and timelines, 3D, running code, chemistry) and pictures (search, generation, framing on the subject, real and generated footage). New bench clips for each new kind. Can overlap with wave 4.
